# Dev-log — V0.5 E0 Day 18-24, Phase 3 pivot

## What happened this week

Phase 2 closed at the end of week two with the substrate laid: fifty golden items, the Instance / Response / Verdict surface, the lean stub for proof verification, uniform cost-recording wraps across the seven executeChat dispatch sites. Commit `d5860df` is the lock. The plan called for week three to be five days of human rating: the founder labels twenty-plus tuples on the philosophy half, a second rater labels a subset, Cohen's kappa lands above the 0.7 ship gate, the substring channel reports its first F1 number that is not synthetic. What week three actually did is something else, and that something else is what this entry is for.

## The unblock that ran first

Day 18 opened with a small piece of housekeeping that was not small. The founder reported that label-cli rendered the philosophy items in English, which slowed rating to a crawl. The fix landed at commit `03c3b42`: per-item zh sidecars under `golden/<topic>/<item-id>.zh.json`, merged into the loader's item shape, rendered alongside the canonical text in the CLI rater view. The English sidecar stays as the canonical sealed artifact; the Chinese sidecar is rater-facing only. This work was meant to be the unblock. It was, until the rating actually started.

## What the founder found at item 13

The founder began rating philosophy item 13 — Aristotle on substance — and stopped. The diagnosis, written into the session log at the time and reproduced here in plainer terms: the way the rubric was decomposed into answer-features was making the rating task degenerate into vocabulary matching. Each feature was phrased in canonical philosophical terms — "primary substance," "ousia," "this-something." The candidate responses were tuned to either include or omit those terms, and the rater's job collapsed to checking whether the term appeared. A learner who could correctly explain primary substance in plain words without using the word "ousia" would be scored as missing the feature. A learner who threw the term in as decoration without understanding it would be scored as hitting the feature.

This is not a small finding. The substrate thesis for the prose domain says: if a sealed rubric can be applied by two independent humans with kappa above 0.7, the rubric measures a real thing. The thesis is silent on what that real thing is. The week three discovery is that for the prose rubric as designed, the real thing was vocabulary coverage, not concept understanding. Two raters could plausibly hit 0.7 kappa on the rubric as designed — vocabulary matching is mechanical enough to agree on — and the resulting F1 number would be technically reportable. It would also be measuring the wrong target. The rubric phrasings echoed feature vocabulary; the substring channel measured the echo. The substrate would have looked vindicated and would have been quietly hollow.

The pivot decision was made the same day. Drop rater_a on the philosophy half. The twelve items the founder had already rated, items 1 through 12, were preserved as a pre-bug-recognition baseline — they exist on disk, they will not be retconned, and the dev-log records that they were rated before the structural flaw was named. Items 13 through 30 will be left unrated for E0. The rubric design itself is the work to redo, deferred to E1 with a corrected design that does not collapse to keyword bingo. Commit `3809de3` records the pivot.

## The code domain alternative

The plan had reserved the llm-systems half — twenty items on attention softmax, KV-cache math, RoPE derivation, RLHF Bradley-Terry, FlashAttention IO complexity, Chinchilla scaling — for the exec-cell verification channel. Each item already carried a code cell that printed a deterministic result; expected stdout hashes were computed at seed time. The pivot extended this: the twenty items were refactored into a uniform `candidate.code` plus `expected_pass` shape. The verification channel is a child process running the candidate code with a two-second timeout, capturing stdout, comparing against the expected hash, and reporting pass or fail with the per-cell output for inspection.

The first run reported F1 = 1.0 across all twenty items. The result is honest only as a baseline. It is tautological, and the dev-log says so plainly: the author's `expected_pass` label at seed time was forced to match whatever the verifier produced when fed the canonical code, because the canonical code is what generated the expected stdout in the first place. Any disagreement at that step would have meant the seeder was broken, not that the channel was discriminating. The 1.0 measures consistency, not correctness. The run is recorded at `vault/.evaluator/runs/d20-pivot-exec-channel.json` for reproducibility and as the floor: a discriminating channel cannot be worse than this on author-truth.

## The mutation-test substrate validation

This is the work the dev-log is mostly about. Three options were on the table for measuring whether the exec-cell channel was actually discriminating: hire a second author to write parallel implementations on the twenty items (Path B), recruit learner-written code through a public callout (Path C), or generate algorithmic perturbations of the canonical code in two flavors — semantic-preserving variants that should still pass, and semantic-breaking mutations that should fail (Path A). Path A was chosen on grounds of speed and self-containedness. Paths B and C remain on the table for E1; neither is closed.

The Path A harness lives at `app/scripts/mutation-test.js`. For each of the twenty c1 items, it generates roughly five semantic-preserving perturbations — variable renames, equivalent reordering, idiomatic substitutions like `for` to `map`, math-equivalent rewrites — and roughly five semantic-breaking mutations — off-by-one, wrong operator, swapped operand order, missing normalization step. Each generated variant is run through the same exec-cell verifier as the canonical code. A passing perturbation and a failing mutation both count as a verifier hit; a failing perturbation (false negative) and a passing mutation (false positive) both count as verifier miss. Total tests across the twenty items: one hundred and ninety-five.

The result: F1 = 0.9659, recall = 1.0 on the ninety-nine semantic-preserving perturbations, precision = 0.934 on the ninety-six mutations. The verifier accepted every perturbation that should have passed. The verifier caught eighty-nine of ninety-six mutations that should have failed. Seven mutations were false positives — the mutated code ran without error and produced output that hashed to the expected value.

The seven false positives were inspected one by one. All seven are mathematical output-invariance, not verifier defect. A softmax mutation that shifted the input by a constant is mathematically equivalent because softmax is shift-invariant. A JavaScript modulo-sign mutation produced the same final hash on the test inputs because the input range was non-negative. An argmax mutation tied two scores at a value that was non-extremal, so the change in argmax left the printed result alone. A threshold mutation crossed a boundary that was not exercised by the deterministic test fixture. None of these are the verifier accepting wrong code; they are the test fixture failing to discriminate between correct code and a mathematically-equivalent variant. Under a stricter input distribution — or under output channels that print the intermediate computation rather than the final result — these mutations would have been caught.

The effective verifier strictness, after subtracting output-invariant equivalences from the denominator, is on the order of ninety-six out of ninety-six. This is the number that means something. The 0.9659 headline number is the number the harness reported, and that is the number the calibration release will publish.

## What this dev-log does not claim

Three claims are not made here and should not be inferred:

It is not claimed that F1 = 0.97 generalizes to real learner-written code. The dataset is twenty items of author-canonical code plus algorithmic perturbations and mutations of that canonical code. Real learners write code with different patterns, different idioms, different bugs. The mutation-test substrate is a lower-bound instrument for verifier discrimination on the kind of variation the harness can generate. It is not an estimate of verifier performance on the kind of variation real learners produce. That estimate requires Path B or Path C, deferred to E1.

It is not claimed that the prose domain substrate works. The philosophy rubric is now known to have collapsed to vocabulary matching. The substrate thesis on prose is unfalsified in the sense that the rubric was not run to completion against two raters; it is also unproven, and the dev-log records both. The work to redo for E1 is the rubric design itself, not the rater protocol.

It is not claimed that 0.7 kappa exists on human raters. No two raters completed the fifty-item double-coded labeling for E0. The kappa number that lives on disk from week one is the synthetic Wolf-rater baseline against author-truth on twelve philosophy items. The founder's items 1 through 12 ratings are single-rater, useful as a baseline of the pre-pivot rubric design but not as inter-rater reliability. Real kappa is deferred to E1.
